0. The mathematical toolkit

Everything later is built from these eight tools. None is difficult on its own; the later "hard" steps are just several of these used one after another.

0.1 Decibels

Intuition. Ratios in this project span a factor of 10,000 (−10 dB to 30 dB). Decibels turn multiplication into addition so such ranges are easy to talk about.

$$x_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10} x,\qquad x = 10^{x_{\text{dB}}/10}.$$

Our number. "MSE > 2 × CRLB" is the same as "MSE/CRLB > 3 dB". The MLE threshold 9.52 dB means E/N0 = 100.952 ≈ 8.95. A "2.6 dB gap" means a factor 100.26 ≈ 1.8 in signal energy.

0.2 Random variables, mean and variance

A random variable X is a quantity whose value depends on chance (here: noise). Its mean (expected value) is the long-run average, its variance the average squared distance from the mean:

$$\mathbb E[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,dx,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X) = \mathbb E\big[(X-\mathbb E X)^2\big] = \mathbb E[X^2] - (\mathbb E X)^2 .$$

Two rules used constantly: linearity $\mathbb E[aX+bY] = a\,\mathbb E X + b\,\mathbb E Y$, and for independent X, Y: $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{var}X+\operatorname{var}Y$, $\operatorname{var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{var}X$.

Proof of var X = E[X²] − (E X)²

Write $m=\mathbb E X$. Expand the square: $(X-m)^2 = X^2 - 2mX + m^2$. Take the mean of each term (linearity): $\mathbb E[X^2] - 2m\,\mathbb E X + m^2 = \mathbb E[X^2] - 2m^2 + m^2 = \mathbb E[X^2]-m^2$. ∎

0.3 Estimators, error, bias, MSE

An estimator $\hat\theta(\mathbf r)$ is any rule that turns the received data $\mathbf r$ into a guess of the unknown $\theta$ (here the delay τ). Its error is $e=\hat\theta-\theta$. Three measures:

$$\text{bias} = \mathbb E[e],\qquad \text{variance} = \operatorname{var}(e),\qquad \text{MSE} = \mathbb E[e^2].$$
Proof: MSE = bias² + variance

This is exactly 0.2 applied to the random variable e: $\operatorname{var}(e) = \mathbb E[e^2] - (\mathbb E e)^2$, so $\mathbb E[e^2] = \operatorname{var}(e) + (\mathbb E e)^2$. ∎

Why we plot MSE, not variance

Below the threshold our estimators are biased (they get pulled toward wrong peaks and window edges). Variance alone would hide that error. MSE counts both parts — it is what a user of the delay estimate actually suffers.

An estimator is unbiased if $\mathbb E[e]=0$ for every true value θ. This matters for the CRLB (Level 3).

0.4 The Gaussian distribution

Thermal noise is modelled as Gaussian: $w\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ has density

$$p(w) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{w^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).$$

Facts we use: (a) a sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, means add, variances add; (b) $\mathbb E[w]=0$, $\mathbb E[w^2]=\sigma^2$; (c) for N independent samples the joint density is the product of the individual densities, so the exponents add:

$$p(w_1,\dots,w_N) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n} w_n^2\Big).$$

(d) Rotation invariance: white Gaussian noise looks the same in every direction. If $\mathbf u$ is a unit vector ($\sum u_n^2=1$), the projection $\sum_n w_n u_n$ is again $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ — because its variance is $\sum_n u_n^2\sigma^2=\sigma^2$. This single fact gives us the detection error in Level 5.

0.5 The Q-function

$Q(x)$ is the probability that a standard Gaussian $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ exceeds x:

$$Q(x) = P(Z > x) = \int_x^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}\,du .$$

Properties: Q decreases from ½ to 0; its derivative is minus the Gaussian density, $Q'(x) = -\phi(x)$ with $\phi(x)=e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$; and if $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ then $P(X>a)=Q(a/\sigma)$ (divide by σ to make it standard).

A small integral we need later

$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty u\,Q(u)\,du=\tfrac14$. Proof by integration by parts with $f=Q(u)$, $g'=u$ (so $g=u^2/2$, $f'=-\phi$): $\int_0^\infty uQ\,du = \big[\tfrac{u^2}{2}Q(u)\big]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty \tfrac{u^2}{2}\phi(u)\,du$. The bracket is 0 at both ends (at ∞ Q decays faster than u² grows). The last integral is half of $\int_0^\infty u^2\phi\,du = \tfrac12\mathbb E[Z^2]=\tfrac12$ (half the line, by symmetry). So the result is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14$. ∎

0.6 Taylor expansion

Near a point a, a smooth function is approximately a polynomial:

$$f(a+x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,x + \tfrac12 f''(a)\,x^2 .$$

We use it three times: the CRLB is about the curvature (second derivative) of the correlation peak; the ZZB's high-SNR limit uses $1-\rho(h)\approx\frac12\beta^2h^2$; the DLL's discriminator is linear near zero error.

0.7 Inner products and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

Treat N samples as a vector. The inner product $\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle=\sum_n a_nb_n$ measures how much two signals "agree"; $\|\mathbf a\|^2=\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf a\rangle$ is the energy. A correlation is just an inner product.

$$\textbf{Cauchy–Schwarz:}\qquad \langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle^2 \le \|\mathbf a\|^2\,\|\mathbf b\|^2,\quad\text{equality iff }\mathbf a\propto\mathbf b.$$
Proof (two lines)

For any number t, $\|\mathbf a - t\mathbf b\|^2\ge0$, i.e. $\|\mathbf a\|^2-2t\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle+t^2\|\mathbf b\|^2\ge0$. A quadratic in t that is never negative has discriminant ≤ 0: $4\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle^2-4\|\mathbf a\|^2\|\mathbf b\|^2\le0$. ∎

The same inequality holds for random variables with $\langle X,Y\rangle=\mathbb E[XY]$: $(\mathbb E[XY])^2\le\mathbb E[X^2]\,\mathbb E[Y^2]$. This is the whole CRLB proof (Level 3).

0.8 Swapping sum/integral and expectation

The mean of a sum (or integral) is the sum (integral) of the means. For non-negative quantities you may always swap the order (Tonelli/Fubini). We use this once, in the ZZB's "tail identity" (Level 5.1).

Level 1

1. The problem and the signal model

1.1 The problem in words

A transmitter sends a known waveform s(t). It arrives late by an unknown delay τ and with noise added. We want to estimate τ. In GNSS, τ × speed of light = distance to the satellite, so a delay error of 0.1 chip on GPS C/A (1 chip ≈ 293 m) is ≈ 29 m of range error. Everything in the project asks: how small can the delay error be, and when does it suddenly become large?

1.2 Chips, BPSK and the pilot

The waveform is built from 64 chips $c_k\in\{+1,-1\}$ (BPSK = binary phase-shift keying: the sign of a pulse carries ±1). The chip sequence is random but fixed and known to the receiver (a "pilot"; ours is seed 2026). We measure time in chips, so the chip duration is 1.

$$s(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{63} c_k\,p(t-k).$$

1.3 The pulse p(t)

A rectangular chip has infinitely sharp edges, hence infinite bandwidth. Level 3 shows that the CRLB is $1/(2(E/N_0)\beta^2)$ with β the bandwidth — a rectangle would give CRLB = 0, a meaningless bound. So we use a raised-cosine pulse with roll-off α = 0.5:

  • Nyquist property: p(0) = 1 and p(k) = 0 at every other integer → chips do not interfere at the sampling instants (checked: max |p(k≠0)| = 3·10⁻¹⁷).
  • Band-limited: its spectrum ends at (1+α)/2 = 0.75 cycles/chip.
  • Truncated to |t| ≤ 6 chips, with a smooth taper over the last chip (a hard cut creates a tiny kink whose derivative spoils the CRLB/ZZB comparison; the taper fixed ZZB/CRLB → 0.997).

1.4 Sampling and the received signal

We take 4 samples per chip ($t_n=n/4$). Sampling theorem: we need more than 2 × 0.75 = 1.5 samples/chip; 4 gives a margin so derivatives (needed by the CRLB and DLL) are accurate.

$$r_n = s(t_n-\tau) + w_n,\qquad w_n\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\ \text{independent.}$$

The delay is applied analytically (we evaluate the formula of s at $t_n-\tau$) — no interpolation, so at high SNR there is no artificial error floor (noise-free MLE error was 0.02 % of the CRLB at 30 dB).

1.5 The SNR: E/N0, and where the "2" comes from

Derivation of E/N0 = Σs²/(2σ²)

In continuous time, a signal of energy E in white noise of two-sided spectral density N0/2 gives a matched-filter output SNR of 2E/N0 (textbook result). In our discrete model the matched filter is the correlation $\sum_n r_n s_n$: its signal part is $\sum s_n^2$, its noise part $\sum w_ns_n$ has variance $\sigma^2\sum s_n^2$ (rule 0.2), so its SNR is $(\sum s_n^2)^2/(\sigma^2\sum s_n^2)=\sum s_n^2/\sigma^2$. Setting the two equal: $\sum s_n^2/\sigma^2 = 2E/N_0$, i.e.

$$\frac{E}{N_0}=\frac{\sum_n s_n^2}{2\sigma^2}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \sigma^2=\frac{\sum_n s_n^2}{2\,E/N_0}.$$

This is exactly sigma2() in sdet/model.py; the notebook checks it (requested 10.0, measured 10.03).

Why total E/N0 and not per-chip? Both bounds depend on the total energy collected. The per-chip value is E/N0 ÷ 64 (≈ −18 dB lower).

1.6 The prior: what we know before looking

τ is drawn uniformly over a window of width $T_a=4$ chips. A uniform variable on a width $T_a$ has variance $T_a^2/12 = 16/12 = 1.33$ chip² (proof: $\int_{-T_a/2}^{T_a/2}x^2\,dx/T_a = T_a^2/12$). This is the error of "knowing nothing but the window" — the ceiling any sensible estimator should stay under. The ZZB needs this prior; the CRLB ignores it.

1.7 Autocorrelation: the shape behind everything

Intuition. Slide a copy of the signal against itself by h and measure how similar it is. If the shifted copy still looks very similar, noise can easily confuse the two delays.

$$\rho(h)=\frac{\sum_n s(t_n)\,s(t_n-h)}{\sum_n s(t_n)^2},\qquad \rho(0)=1.$$

Our pilot: the main lobe reaches zero at ±1.34 chips; beyond 1.5 chips the side-lobes are at most 0.13.

  • The curvature at the top (how sharp the peak is) sets the small-error precision → CRLB.
  • The side-lobes and the window width set how likely a far-away wrong delay is → threshold, ZZB.

1.8 Gabor (RMS) bandwidth β²

$$\beta^2=\frac{\sum_n s'(t_n)^2}{\sum_n s(t_n)^2}\quad\Big(=\frac{\int(2\pi f)^2|S(f)|^2df}{\int|S(f)|^2df}\Big).$$

It measures how fast the signal changes — "steepness per unit energy". Ours: 2.448 /chip² (time domain) vs 2.447 (frequency domain): the two formulas agree to 0.05 %. Over 400 random pilots β² ranges 1.77–4.27, which is why every method in a comparison must use the same pilot.

Link between ρ and β²: ρ″(0) = −β²

Differentiate ρ twice with respect to h (only the shifted copy depends on h): $\rho''(h)=\sum_n s(t_n)s''(t_n-h)/\sum s^2$. At h = 0: $\rho''(0)=\sum s\,s''/\sum s^2$. Integration by parts (sum ≈ integral; s vanishes at both ends): $\int s\,s''\,dt=[s\,s']-\int s'^2\,dt=-\int s'^2\,dt$. Hence $\rho''(0)=-\beta^2$. Also ρ′(0) = 0 (ρ is even, maximal at 0). By Taylor (0.6):

$$1-\rho(h)\approx\tfrac12\beta^2h^2\quad\text{for small }h.$$

We will need this in the ZZB's high-SNR limit.

Level 2

2. The estimators: maximum likelihood and MMSE

2.1 The likelihood idea

Intuition. For each candidate delay θ, ask: "if the true delay were θ, how probable is the data I actually received?" Pick the θ that makes the data most probable. That number, as a function of θ, is the likelihood $p(\mathbf r;\theta)$.

2.2 Deriving the MLE (three lines)

If the delay is θ, the noise must have been $w_n=r_n-s(t_n-\theta)$. Write $s_\theta[n]=s(t_n-\theta)$. By 0.4(c):

$$p(\mathbf r;\theta)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_n\big(r_n-s_\theta[n]\big)^2\Big).$$
Step by step

Step 1 — take the log (log is increasing, so the maximiser does not change): $\ln p=-\tfrac N2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_n(r_n-s_\theta[n])^2$.

Step 2 — expand the square: $\sum(r_n-s_\theta)^2=\sum r_n^2-2\sum r_ns_\theta+\sum s_\theta^2$.

Step 3 — drop what does not depend on θ (the first log term and $\sum r_n^2$), and multiply by σ² > 0: maximising ln p is the same as maximising

$$J(\theta)=\sum_n r_n\,s(t_n-\theta)\;-\;\tfrac12\sum_n s(t_n-\theta)^2,\qquad \hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\arg\max_\theta J(\theta).$$

Meaning. The first term is the correlation of the data with a replica shifted by θ — a matched filter. The second term is half the replica energy; it is almost constant in θ, but we keep it so the estimator is the exact MLE. σ² dropped out: the MLE needs no knowledge of the SNR.

In code: J = r @ templates.T - half_energy (one row of templates per candidate θ).

2.3 Implementation: a grid, then a parabola

We evaluate J on a grid with step 1/64 chip over the prior window, take the best grid point, then fit a parabola through it and its two neighbours to land between grid points.

Derivation of the parabolic refinement

Put the best point at x = 0 and its neighbours at x = ±1 (units of the grid step), with values $J_-,J_0,J_+$. Fit $y=ax^2+bx+c$: at x = 0, $c=J_0$; at ±1, $a\pm b+c=J_\pm$. Subtracting: $b=(J_+-J_-)/2$; adding: $a=(J_++J_--2J_0)/2$. The top of the parabola is where $y'=2ax+b=0$:

$$x^*=-\frac{b}{2a}=\frac12\,\frac{J_--J_+}{J_--2J_0+J_+}.$$

Exactly the line delta = 0.5 * (jm - jp) / den in _ml_from_objective. We require den < 0 (a maximum) and clip $x^*$ to ±½.

2.4 Why the MLE reaches the CRLB at high SNR (preview of Level 3)

At high SNR the maximum is near the true τ, J is smooth there, and the estimate solves $J'(\hat\theta)=0$. Linearising (Level 3.6) gives an error $e=-\sum w_ns'_n/\sum s_n'^2$, whose variance is exactly the CRLB. Our check: MSE/CRLB = 0.997 averaged over 14–30 dB.

2.5 The MMSE (posterior-mean) estimator

Intuition. The MLE picks the single highest peak — it commits. If two peaks are almost equally high, a smarter estimator hedges: it averages candidate delays weighted by how believable each is.

Step by step

Bayes' rule: $p(\theta\mid\mathbf r)\propto p(\mathbf r;\theta)\,p(\theta)$. With a uniform prior, $p(\theta)$ is constant on the window, so the posterior ∝ the likelihood there.

Use Step 2 of 2.2: $p(\mathbf r;\theta)\propto\exp\!\big(-\frac1{2\sigma^2}[\sum r^2-2\sum rs_\theta+\sum s_\theta^2]\big) \propto\exp\!\big(J(\theta)/\sigma^2\big)$ (the $\sum r^2$ part is a constant factor).

Posterior mean on the grid: with weights $w_i=\exp(J(\theta_i)/\sigma^2)$,

$$\hat\theta_{\text{MMSE}}=\frac{\sum_i\theta_i\,w_i}{\sum_i w_i}.$$

(In code we subtract max J before exponentiating, to avoid overflow — it cancels in the ratio.) Note: unlike the MLE, the MMSE needs σ², i.e. it needs to know the SNR.

Why the posterior mean minimises the MSE

For any guess c made after seeing r: $\mathbb E[(\theta-c)^2\mid\mathbf r]=\operatorname{var}(\theta\mid\mathbf r)+(\mathbb E[\theta\mid\mathbf r]-c)^2$ (this is 0.2/0.3 applied to the posterior). The first term does not depend on c; the second is zero exactly when $c=\mathbb E[\theta\mid\mathbf r]$. Averaging over r keeps the inequality. ∎

Why we added it. The brief does not ask for it (we label it an extension). It answers a key question: is the gap between the MLE and the ZZB because the bound is loose, or because the MLE is sub-optimal? The MMSE is the best any estimator can do on average, so MMSE − ZZB is pure bound looseness (2.59 dB) and MLE − MMSE is pure estimator sub-optimality (0.85 dB).

2.6 Why the MLE can be worse than knowing nothing

On pure noise the MLE's MSE was 3.09 chip², more than the prior variance 1.33. Reason: with no signal, J is just a random noise curve; its maximum is often at a window edge (20 % of the time exactly on an edge), and edges are 2 chips from the centre (squared error up to 4). The MMSE, by contrast, averages over the window and tends to the centre (error ≈ the prior variance). This is a nice illustration that "most likely" ≠ "smallest error".

Level 3

3. The Cramér–Rao lower bound (CRLB)

3.1 Intuition first

If the likelihood has a sharp peak, a small change in θ makes the data much less likely, so the data "pins down" θ precisely. If the peak is flat, many θ explain the data equally well. The CRLB turns "sharpness of the peak" into a number: the Fisher information I(θ). The bound says variance ≥ 1/I.

3.2 Two ingredients: the score and its mean

The score is the slope of the log-likelihood: $S(\mathbf r;\theta)=\partial\ln p(\mathbf r;\theta)/\partial\theta$.

Fact A: the score has mean zero

$\mathbb E[S]=\int\frac{\partial_\theta p}{p}\,p\,d\mathbf r=\int\partial_\theta p\,d\mathbf r=\partial_\theta\!\int p\,d\mathbf r=\partial_\theta 1=0.$ (Line by line: derivative of a log is derivative over the function; the p's cancel; swap derivative and integral; a density integrates to 1.)

The Fisher information is the variance of the score: $I(\theta)=\mathbb E[S^2]$.

3.3 The proof of the CRLB

Fact B: for an unbiased estimator, E[(θ̂ − θ)·S] = 1

Unbiased means $\int\hat\theta(\mathbf r)\,p(\mathbf r;\theta)\,d\mathbf r=\theta$ for every θ. Differentiate both sides with respect to θ ($\hat\theta(\mathbf r)$ does not depend on θ): $\int\hat\theta\,\partial_\theta p\,d\mathbf r=1$, i.e. $\mathbb E[\hat\theta S]=1$. Subtract $\theta\,\mathbb E[S]=0$ (Fact A): $\mathbb E[(\hat\theta-\theta)S]=1$.

The CRLB itself

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz (0.7) to the random variables $\hat\theta-\theta$ and S:

$$1=\big(\mathbb E[(\hat\theta-\theta)S]\big)^2\le\mathbb E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]\;\mathbb E[S^2]=\operatorname{var}(\hat\theta)\cdot I(\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{var}(\hat\theta)\ge\frac1{I(\theta)}.\ \ ∎$$

Equality needs $\hat\theta-\theta\propto S$ — possible in our problem only approximately, at high SNR.

3.4 Computing it for our model

Step by step

Score. From 2.2, $\ln p=\text{const}-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum(r_n-s_\theta[n])^2$. Differentiate (chain rule; $\partial_\theta s(t_n-\theta)=-s'(t_n-\theta)$): $S=\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum_n(r_n-s_\theta[n])\,\partial_\theta s_\theta[n]=-\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum_n w_n\,s'(t_n-\theta)$, using $r_n-s_\theta[n]=w_n$ at the true θ.

Fisher information. $I=\mathbb E[S^2]=\frac1{\sigma^4}\sum_n\sum_m\mathbb E[w_nw_m]s'_ns'_m$. Noise samples are independent, so $\mathbb E[w_nw_m]=\sigma^2$ if n = m and 0 otherwise: $I=\frac{1}{\sigma^4}\sigma^2\sum_n s_n'^2=\frac{\sum s_n'^2}{\sigma^2}$.

Substitute σ² from 1.5, $\sigma^2=\sum s^2/(2E/N_0)$, and the definition of β² (1.8):

$$\text{CRLB}=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum s_n'^2}=\frac{\sum s_n^2}{2(E/N_0)\sum s_n'^2}=\frac{1}{2\,(E/N_0)\,\beta^2}\quad[\text{chip}^2].$$

Our numbers (β² = 2.448): at 10 dB, CRLB = 1/(2·10·2.448) = 0.0204 chip² (RMS error 0.14 chip); at 20 dB, 0.00204 chip² (RMS 0.045 chip). Every 10 dB of SNR divides the CRLB by 10 — a straight line on a log plot.

Reading the formula: more energy (E/N0) or a steeper signal (β²) → smaller error. That is why bandwidth matters in ranging systems.

3.5 Why the CRLB fails at low SNR — three separate reasons

  1. It assumes unbiasedness. Below threshold our estimators are biased (−0.47 chip at 2 dB for a delay near the window edge), so the bound does not apply to them.
  2. It is local. It only uses the curvature at the true peak, never the side-lobes or a far-away wrong peak — exactly where gross errors come from.
  3. It ignores the prior. Below ≈ −8 dB the CRLB exceeds Ta²/12 = 1.33 chip², i.e. it "allows" more error than simply guessing the window centre. (Solve 1/(2·x·2.448) = 1.33 → x = 0.153 = −8.1 dB.)

3.6 Bonus: why the MLE attains the CRLB, and why any other linear rule is worse

Linearising the MLE

At high SNR, $\hat\theta=\tau+e$ with e small and $J'(\hat\theta)=0$. With $r=s_\tau+w$ and the energy term constant, $J'(\theta)=-\sum_n r_n s'(t_n-\theta)$. Taylor in e around τ: $J'(\tau)=-\sum s\,s'-\sum w\,s'=-\sum w\,s'$ (since $\sum s\,s'=\frac12\sum(s^2)'=0$), and $J''(\tau)\approx\sum s\,s''=-\sum s'^2$ (integration by parts, as in 1.8). So $0=J'(\tau)+eJ''(\tau)$ gives

$$e=-\frac{\sum_n w_n s_n'}{\sum_n s_n'^2},\qquad\operatorname{var}(e)=\frac{\sigma^2\sum s'^2}{(\sum s'^2)^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum s'^2}=\text{CRLB}.$$
General lesson (used again for the DLL)

Any estimator that, near the truth, forms $e=\sum w_ng_n/\sum g_ns_n'$ with some weighting g has $\operatorname{var}(e)=\sigma^2\|g\|^2/\langle g,s'\rangle^2\ge\sigma^2/\|s'\|^2$ by Cauchy–Schwarz, with equality only if g ∝ s′. The MLE uses g = s′ (optimal). The DLL's early-minus-late uses a slightly different g, which is why its locked error is 1.07 × CRLB.

Level 4

4. The threshold effect, and how we measure it

4.1 Where gross errors come from — one calculation

Intuition. J(θ) = (signal correlation) + (noise). At the true delay the signal part is a tall peak. At a far-away delay the signal part is small, but the noise wiggles J up and down. Occasionally a noise wiggle somewhere else is taller than the true peak, and the MLE jumps there: a gross error.

Probability that one particular wrong delay τ + h beats the truth

With $r=s_\tau+w$: $J(\tau)-J(\tau+h)=\underbrace{\textstyle\sum s^2\,(1-\rho(h))}_{\text{signal margin}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_n w_n\,(s_\tau[n]-s_{\tau+h}[n])}_{\text{noise}}$ (the energy terms cancel because shifting does not change energy). The noise term is Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2\|s_\tau-s_{\tau+h}\|^2=\sigma^2\cdot2\sum s^2(1-\rho)$ (expand the square as in 5.3). The wrong delay wins when the noise is below minus the margin:

$$P=Q\!\left(\frac{\sum s^2(1-\rho)}{\sigma\sqrt{2\sum s^2(1-\rho)}}\right)=Q\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sum s^2}{2\sigma^2}(1-\rho)}\right)=Q\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{E}{N_0}\,(1-\rho(h))}\right).$$

Remember this expression — it reappears as $P_{\min}$ in the ZZB.

Why the failure is abrupt. Q falls off like $e^{-x^2/2}$, so the chance of a jump collapses quickly as SNR grows. But when a jump happens it costs an error of order 1–2 chips (squared: 1–4 chip²), while the CRLB is only ≈ 0.02 chip² at 10 dB. So even a 1 % jump probability with a 1.5-chip error adds 0.01 × 2.25 ≈ 0.022 chip² — about the CRLB itself, doubling the MSE. That is why our threshold sits near 9.5 dB: notebook 01 measured jump probabilities of 0.8 % at 10 dB, 2.1 % at 8 dB, 8.7 % at 6 dB.

4.3 Two threshold definitions (fixed before looking at results)

  • MSE criterion: the highest SNR at which MSE/CRLB first exceeds 2 (3 dB). Scanning down from high SNR, we interpolate between the two grid points that bracket the crossing, on a log scale.
  • Gross-error criterion: the highest SNR at which P(|e| > 0.5 chip) first exceeds 1 %.

Two criteria because they answer different questions: MSE weighs big errors heavily; the gross-error rate counts how often you fail regardless of how badly. The DLL-vs-MLE answer differs between them (Level 7).

4.4 Monte Carlo, and why we need confidence intervals

We estimate MSE by averaging $e^2$ over N independent trials. Its standard error is std($e^2$)/√N. Near the threshold $e^2$ is heavy-tailed (mostly ≈ 0.02, occasionally ≈ 4), so a few unlucky trials move the MSE a lot, and the threshold can shift by a dB. Hence 20,000 trials per point and a 0.5-dB grid in notebook 06.

The bootstrap, step by step
  1. At every SNR, draw N trials with replacement from the N errors we have (a "resample").
  2. Recompute the threshold from the resampled errors.
  3. Repeat 200–300 times → 200–300 threshold values.
  4. The 2.5 % and 97.5 % percentiles form the 95 % confidence interval.

Idea: the resamples mimic "running the experiment again". For a difference (e.g. DLL − MLE) we take the difference in each resample; if the interval excludes 0, the difference is significant.

An honest caveat you should know

The bootstrap captures trial-to-trial noise, not the error of interpolating across a coarse SNR grid. Notebook 05's quick table (2-dB grid) therefore has intervals that are too narrow — e.g. DLL gross threshold 8.0 dB there vs 8.81 dB [8.54, 9.06] in notebook 06. Always quote notebook 06.

Level 5 — the heart of the project

5. The Ziv–Zakai bound (ZZB), derived from scratch

5.0 The big idea in one paragraph

Estimating a delay well implies being able to tell two nearby delays apart. If your estimate is usually within h/2 of the truth, you could use it to decide "is the delay φ or φ + h?". But detection theory gives the smallest possible error rate of any such decision, $P_{\min}$. So no estimator can have small errors more often than that limit allows. Summing this argument over every separation h gives a lower bound on the MSE. Because it looks at all separations — including large ones — it sees gross errors, unlike the CRLB.

The derivation has five steps. Learn them in order; each is short.

5.1 Step 1 — MSE as an integral of tail probabilities

The tail identity

For a non-negative random variable X: $X^2=\int_0^X 2x\,dx=\int_0^\infty 2x\,\mathbf 1\{X\ge x\}\,dx$ (the indicator is 1 while x ≤ X and 0 after). Take the mean of both sides and swap mean and integral (0.8); the mean of an indicator is a probability:

$$\mathbb E[X^2]=\int_0^\infty 2x\,P(X\ge x)\,dx .$$

Apply it to X = |e| and substitute x = h/2 (so dx = dh/2):

$$\text{MSE}=\mathbb E[e^2]=\int_0^\infty \frac h2\;P\!\left(|e|\ge \frac h2\right)dh .$$

Meaning. The MSE is a weighted sum, over all error sizes, of "how often is the error at least that large". Big errors carry big weights (h). Now we only need a lower bound on $P(|e|\ge h/2)$.

5.2 Step 2 — the best possible detector between two known signals

Set-up. Two equally likely hypotheses: $H_0:\mathbf r=\mathbf s_0+\mathbf w$ and $H_1:\mathbf r=\mathbf s_1+\mathbf w$.

Derivation of P_min = Q(d / 2σ)

(a) Best rule. With equal priors, the error-minimising rule picks the more likely hypothesis. The likelihoods are ∝ $\exp(-\|\mathbf r-\mathbf s_i\|^2/2\sigma^2)$, so: pick the signal closer to r.

(b) When does it err under H₀? When r is closer to s₁: $\|\mathbf r-\mathbf s_1\|^2<\|\mathbf r-\mathbf s_0\|^2$. Put $\mathbf r=\mathbf s_0+\mathbf w$ and $\mathbf d=\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0$, $d=\|\mathbf d\|$: $\|\mathbf w-\mathbf d\|^2<\|\mathbf w\|^2 \iff \|\mathbf w\|^2-2\langle\mathbf w,\mathbf d\rangle+d^2<\|\mathbf w\|^2\iff\langle\mathbf w,\mathbf d/d\rangle>d/2.$

(c) Probability. $\langle\mathbf w,\mathbf d/d\rangle$ is the noise projected on a unit vector, so it is $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ (0.4 d). Hence $P(\text{error}\mid H_0)=Q\big(\frac{d/2}{\sigma}\big)$. By symmetry the same under H₁, so

$$P_{\min}=Q\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_0\|}{2\sigma}\right).$$

Picture: two points at distance d; the decision line is halfway; you err when noise pushes you more than d/2 toward the wrong side.

5.3 Step 3 — our two signals are the pilot at delays φ and φ + h

Distance between shifted copies

$d^2=\sum_n\big(s(t_n-\phi-h)-s(t_n-\phi)\big)^2=\sum s_{\phi+h}^2+\sum s_\phi^2-2\sum s_\phi s_{\phi+h}=2\sum s^2-2\rho(h)\sum s^2=2\sum s^2\,(1-\rho(h)).$

Then $\frac{d}{2\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{2\sum s^2(1-\rho)}{4\sigma^2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum s^2}{2\sigma^2}(1-\rho)}=\sqrt{\tfrac{E}{N_0}(1-\rho(h))}$ (using 1.5), so

$$P_{\min}(h)=Q\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{E}{N_0}\,\big(1-\rho(h)\big)}\right).$$

(It does not depend on φ — we checked: it varies by ≈ 2·10⁻⁵. Notice it is exactly the "wrong peak wins" probability of 4.1.)

Our numbers. For a separation beyond the main lobe (ρ ≈ 0): at 0 dB, $P_{\min}=Q(1)=0.16$; at 6 dB (E/N0 ≈ 4), $Q(2)=0.023$; at 20 dB, $Q(10)\approx10^{-23}$. Large errors are possible at low SNR and impossible at high SNR.

5.4 Step 4 — any estimator can be turned into a detector

Lower bound on P(|e| ≥ h/2)

(a) The detector. Given φ and h, decide "τ = φ + h" if $\hat\theta\ge\phi+h/2$, otherwise "τ = φ". If truly τ = φ, it errs iff $e=\hat\theta-\phi\ge h/2$. If truly τ = φ + h, it errs iff $e<-h/2$. With equal priors its error rate is the average of the two, and no detector beats $P_{\min}$:

$$P(e\ge\tfrac h2\mid\tau=\phi)+P(e\le-\tfrac h2\mid\tau=\phi+h)\;\ge\;2P_{\min}(h).\qquad(\ast)$$

(b) Average over the prior. Split $|e|\ge h/2$ into $e\ge h/2$ or $e\le-h/2$: $P(|e|\ge\tfrac h2)=\int p(\tau)P(e\ge\tfrac h2|\tau)d\tau+\int p(\tau)P(e\le-\tfrac h2|\tau)d\tau$. In the second integral rename τ = φ + h. With the uniform prior, p = 1/Ta on the window [0, Ta].

(c) Keep only φ where both φ and φ + h are inside the window (φ from 0 to Ta − h). The dropped parts are ≥ 0, so this can only make the right side smaller:

$$P(|e|\ge\tfrac h2)\;\ge\;\frac1{T_a}\int_0^{T_a-h}\big[\,(\ast)\text{'s left side}\,\big]d\phi\;\ge\;\frac{T_a-h}{T_a}\,2P_{\min}(h)=2\Big(1-\frac h{T_a}\Big)P_{\min}(h).$$

5.5 Step 5 — put it together

The ZZB

Insert 5.4 into 5.1. For h > Ta the bound in 5.4 is 0 (no room for two delays), so the integral stops at Ta:

$$\text{MSE}\;\ge\;\int_0^{T_a}\frac h2\cdot2\Big(1-\frac h{T_a}\Big)P_{\min}(h)\,dh=\boxed{\int_0^{T_a}h\,\Big(1-\frac h{T_a}\Big)\,Q\!\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{E}{N_0}(1-\rho(h))}\right)dh}$$

This is the bound in sdet/bounds.py → zzb() (plus valley filling, next).

Read each factor: h — big errors weigh more; (1 − h/Ta) — the fraction of the window where a pair at distance h fits; $P_{\min}(h)$ — how confusable delays h apart are at this SNR.

5.6 Valley filling (Bell et al. 1997)

$P(|e|\ge h/2)$ can only decrease as h grows. So for any η ≥ 0, the bound at h + η is also a bound at h. Taking the best one, replace $f(h)=(1-h/T_a)P_{\min}(h)$ by $\mathcal V\{f\}(h)=\max_{\eta\ge0}f(h+\eta)$ — it fills in dips of f caused by side-lobes. The bound can only get tighter. For our pilot the side-lobes are small (≤ 0.13) and valley filling changes the ZZB by < 0.01 %. In code: a running maximum from the right, np.maximum.accumulate(f[:, ::-1])[:, ::-1].

5.7 Limit 1 — no signal: ZZB → Ta²/12

Step by step

E/N0 → 0 gives $Q(0)=\tfrac12$ for every h. Then $\text{ZZB}=\tfrac12\int_0^{T_a}\big(h-\tfrac{h^2}{T_a}\big)dh=\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{T_a^2}2-\tfrac{T_a^2}3\Big)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{T_a^2}{6}=\tfrac{T_a^2}{12},$ the prior variance. (f is decreasing here, so valley filling changes nothing.)

Meaning: with no data the best you can do is guess the window centre; the bound stays finite, unlike the CRLB. Numerically: 1.3323 vs 1.3333 (the tiny gap is the finite numerical SNR used).

5.8 Limit 2 — strong signal: ZZB → CRLB

Step by step

(a) At high SNR, $P_{\min}(h)$ is negligible unless h is tiny. For tiny h use 1.8: $1-\rho(h)\approx\tfrac12\beta^2h^2$, so $P_{\min}(h)\approx Q(ah)$ with $a=\beta\sqrt{(E/N_0)/2}$.

(b) For tiny h, $1-h/T_a\approx1$, and extending the upper limit from Ta to ∞ adds only negligible area: $\text{ZZB}\approx\int_0^\infty h\,Q(ah)\,dh$.

(c) Substitute u = ah (h = u/a, dh = du/a): $\int_0^\infty\frac ua Q(u)\frac{du}a=\frac1{a^2}\int_0^\infty uQ(u)\,du=\frac1{4a^2}$ (box in 0.5).

(d) $\frac1{4a^2}=\frac{1}{4\beta^2(E/N_0)/2}=\frac1{2(E/N_0)\beta^2}=\text{CRLB}.$

Numerically: ZZB/CRLB = 0.9971 at 40 dB (→ 1). Getting both limits right is our main correctness check.

5.9 What happens in between — the integrand picture

At 20 dB the integrand $h(1-h/T_a)P_{\min}(h)$ lives only at h ≪ 1 chip: a local, CRLB-like picture. At 0 dB $P_{\min}\approx0.16$ even at h = 2–3 chips, so large separations contribute: that is the bound "seeing" gross errors. The ZZB leaves the CRLB (ZZB > 2 × CRLB) at 5.95 dB.

5.10 Why the ZZB is optimistic by ≈ 2.6 dB

Step 5.4 compares the truth with one alternative delay at a time (a binary test). A real estimator must reject all wrong delays in the window at once (an M-ary test): the chance that any of many noise peaks beats the truth is larger than the chance for one particular one. The ZZB never "adds up" these competitors, so it underestimates gross errors and puts the threshold too low in SNR. We measured this looseness directly against the best estimator: MMSE threshold − ZZB threshold = 8.54 − 5.95 = 2.59 dB.

If the professor digs

"Your ZZB is 3.6 dB below your MLE threshold — is it wrong?" No: it reproduces both limits analytically and numerically, an independent re-implementation agrees to 0.12 %, and the MLE is above it at every SNR. 2.6 dB of the gap is the bound's known looseness (5.10); 0.85 dB is the MLE's sub-optimality (it commits to one peak; the MMSE hedges).

Level 6

6. The delay-locked loop (DLL)

6.1 Why a loop at all

The batch MLE re-searches the whole window every time — expensive. A real receiver instead tracks: it keeps a current estimate θ, measures how wrong it is using just two correlators, and corrects a fraction of that error, block after block. That is feedback, like a thermostat.

6.2 Early and late correlators, and the S-curve

Let the tracking error be ε = θ − τ (positive = our estimate is too late). Correlate the data with an early replica (shifted d/2 earlier than θ) and a late one (d/2 later), with spacing d = 0.5 chip. With R the signal's (unnormalised) autocorrelation, ignoring noise:

$$E=R(\varepsilon-\tfrac d2),\qquad L=R(\varepsilon+\tfrac d2),\qquad D(\varepsilon)=E-L.$$
Why E − L is proportional to the error (Taylor, 0.6)

Expand both around ε = 0: $R(\pm\tfrac d2+\varepsilon)\approx R(\pm\tfrac d2)+R'(\pm\tfrac d2)\,\varepsilon$. R is even, so $R(-\tfrac d2)=R(\tfrac d2)$ and R′ is odd, so $R'(-\tfrac d2)=-R'(\tfrac d2)$. Subtract:

$$D(\varepsilon)\approx\big[R(-\tfrac d2)-R(\tfrac d2)\big]+\big[R'(-\tfrac d2)-R'(\tfrac d2)\big]\varepsilon=-2R'(\tfrac d2)\,\varepsilon .$$

On the main lobe R is decreasing, so $R'(d/2)<0$ and $k=-2R'(d/2)>0$: D has the same sign as ε, and D/k ≈ ε.

The S-curve is D(ε)/k plotted over ε. Ours: slope 1.000 at zero; peak 0.44 at ε = 0.70 chip; correct sign up to ±1.80 chips — the pull-in range. Inside it the loop is pushed back toward ε = 0; outside it, it is not.

Why d = 0.5? A trade-off: a narrow spacing approximates the derivative s′ better (less noise, closer to the CRLB) but shrinks the linear region; a wide one tolerates bigger errors but is noisier. 0.5 chip is a standard compromise.

6.3 The loop update and its noise — every step

Each update uses one block of 4 chips: $\theta_{j+1}=\theta_j-K\,(D_j-b_j)/\bar k$. In the linear region the corrected discriminator measures the error plus noise: $(D_j-b_j)/\bar k\approx\varepsilon_j+n_j$, with $n_j$ zero-mean, variance $\sigma_n^2$, independent from block to block. Subtract τ from both sides:

$$\varepsilon_{j+1}=(1-K)\,\varepsilon_j-K\,n_j .$$
(a) Without noise: the error shrinks geometrically

$\varepsilon_j=(1-K)^j\varepsilon_0$. With K = 2/17 ≈ 0.118, the error falls by a factor e every ≈ 1/K ≈ 8.5 updates. Noise-free check: final error ≈ 10⁻⁷ chip.

(b) With noise: the steady-state variance

In steady state $\operatorname{var}(\varepsilon_{j+1})=\operatorname{var}(\varepsilon_j)=V$. Since $n_j$ is independent of $\varepsilon_j$, variances add (0.2): $V=(1-K)^2V+K^2\sigma_n^2$. Solve: $V\,[1-(1-K)^2]=K^2\sigma_n^2$, and $1-(1-K)^2=2K-K^2=K(2-K)$, so

$$V=\frac{K\,\sigma_n^2}{2-K}=\frac{\sigma_n^2}{N_{\text{eff}}},\qquad N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{2-K}{K}.$$

Meaning: the loop behaves like averaging $N_{\text{eff}}$ independent blocks. With K = 2/17: $N_{\text{eff}}=(32/17)/(2/17)=16$ blocks × 4 chips = 64 chips — the same data length as the batch MLE. That is exactly why we chose K = 2/(N_eff + 1) = 2/17 (the familiar exponential-moving-average formula).

(c) Compare with the CRLB

One 4-chip block has 1/16 of the batch energy, and CRLB ∝ 1/energy, so an ideal per-block measurement has $\sigma_n^2\approx16\cdot\text{CRLB}_{64}$. Then $V\approx16\cdot\text{CRLB}_{64}/16=\text{CRLB}_{64}$. Measured: 1.04–1.09 × CRLB. The exact linear theory gives 1.071: early-minus-late weights the data with a shape that is not exactly s′, and by Cauchy–Schwarz (3.6) any weighting other than s′ is worse. This is by design of K — it is not a bound.

Loop bandwidth. For this first-order loop $B_L=\frac{K}{2(2-K)T}$ with T = 4 chips per update: $B_L=\frac{1}{32\cdot4}=0.0078$/chip, and $1/(2B_L)=64$ chips — the same "averaging time" stated differently. Each run is 160 updates = 10 N_eff = 640 chips, long enough to forget the starting error.

6.5 Loss of lock — the DLL's own failure mode

If noise pushes ε beyond the pull-in range, the discriminator no longer points home: the loop drifts, and its MSE grows with observation time instead of settling. At 0 dB, 54 % of runs end with a gross error: 12 % because acquisition started outside the pull-in range, 42 % because the loop lost lock while tracking. A batch MLE has no such mechanism — each block is a fresh estimate.

6.6 Is the DLL-vs-MLE comparison fair?

Same pilot (repeated), same noise model, same SNR axis (batch E/N0 per 64 chips). But the DLL sees 640 chips — 10× the energy. So the 64-chip ZZB is not a bound for the DLL; we also computed the ZZB for the DLL's own 640 chips, and the DLL stays above it at every SNR, as it must.

8. The mathematics of the proposal

PROPOSED Nothing in this level has results yet. Learn it so you can explain the plan and defend its feasibility.

8.1 A moving delay

Real delays drift. Simplest model, a random walk: $\tau_k=\tau_{k-1}+u_k$, $u_k\sim\mathcal N(0,q)$. Now the question is how well we can track τ_k over time, and where trackers lose information.

8.2 The scalar Kalman filter in two steps

Predict, then update

Predict: before the new block, our uncertainty grows by the drift: $P^-=P+q$.

Update: the new block gives a measurement of τ with noise variance R. Combining two independent Gaussian estimates, information (1/variance) adds: $\frac1{P^+}=\frac1{P^-}+\frac1R$, and the new estimate moves toward the measurement by the gain $K=\frac{P^-}{P^-+R}$: $\hat x^+=\hat x^-+K(z-\hat x^-)$.

The DLL is this filter with a fixed gain: its update θ ← θ − K·D/k is $\hat x^+=\hat x^-+K(z-\hat x^-)$ with "measurement" $z=\theta-D/k$. So a DLL whose K equals the steady-state Kalman gain is optimal in its linear regime (H1).

8.3 The posterior CRB (PCRB) recursion and its steady state

The Bayesian analogue of the CRLB for tracking has the same predict/update form in terms of information $J_k=1/P_k$, with $J_D=1/R$ the Fisher information of one block:

$$J_k=J_D+\frac{1}{q+1/J_{k-1}} .$$
Steady state

Set $P_k=P_{k-1}=P$: $\frac1P=\frac1R+\frac1{q+P}$ ⇒ $P=\frac{R(q+P)}{R+q+P}$ ⇒ $P(R+q+P)=Rq+RP$ ⇒ $P^2+qP-qR=0$. Take the positive root:

$$P_\infty=\tfrac12\Big(-q+\sqrt{q^2+4qR}\Big),\qquad K_\infty=\frac{P_\infty+q}{P_\infty+q+R}.$$

8.4 The optimal tracker on a grid

The state is one number, so the exact Bayes filter can be computed on a grid of delays: predict = convolve the current posterior with the Gaussian drift kernel N(0, q); update = multiply by the block likelihood ∝ exp(J(θ)/σ²) (Level 2.5); normalise. No particle-filter approximation is needed.

8.5 Mean time to lose lock (MTLL) as a Markov chain

Discretise the tracking error into bins. From the measured S-curve and the noise level, compute the probability of moving from each bin to each other bin in one update (matrix Q, restricted to the "locked" bins). Leaving the pull-in range is an absorbing "lost" state. The expected number of updates until absorption, t, solves $(I-Q)\,\mathbf t=\mathbf 1$.

8.6 The four hypotheses, in one line each

  • H1 Above threshold, the DLL with K∞ and the grid Bayes tracker both reach the PCRB.
  • H2 The DLL leaves the bound earlier; conditioning on "still locked" removes most of the excess.
  • H3 The Markov chain built on our S-curve predicts the MTLL and the DLL's threshold.
  • H4 The posterior probability of |error| > 0.5 chip warns of loss of lock earlier than a classical lock detector (compared by ROC curves).
If the professor digs

"What if H2 fails?" Then the DLL's extra loss is not mainly loss of lock but its sub-optimal (fixed-gain, linear) processing even while locked — still a clear, publishable decomposition. Every hypothesis has a useful negative outcome.
